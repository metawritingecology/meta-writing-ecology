## Minimal Formulation

Responsibility Alignment describes the condition in which responsibility corresponds to actual ownership, legitimate scope, sufficient authority, process visibility, operational capacity, and accountable position.

In compressed form:

```text
capacity
≠
ownership

visibility
≠
responsibility

assistance
≠
transfer
```

Core principle:

```text
responsibility should not expand merely because capacity is present
```

Operationally:

```text
problem appears
→ recognizing node becomes visible
→ system pulls responsibility toward capacity
→ contribution boundary blurs
→ burden concentrates
→ alignment requires mapping origin, ownership, authority, visibility, capacity, and accountability
```

Aligned involvement can take three forms:

```text
directional assistance
scoped contribution
full ownership
```

The analytical failure occurs when:

```text
directional assistance
→ treated as scoped contribution

scoped contribution
→ treated as full ownership

capacity
→ treated as obligation
```

## Core Characteristics

The Responsibility Alignment Model treats responsibility as a structural relation rather than a personal trait, emotional boundary, moral slogan, or simple job title.

Its core characteristics include:

- **Responsibility alignment** — responsibility corresponds to ownership, scope, authority, visibility, capacity, burden, and accountability.
- **Responsibility drift** — responsibility moves toward the node most able to recognize, absorb, stabilize, repair, or contain a problem.
- **Capacity is not ownership** — the ability to solve or stabilize a problem does not mean the problem structurally belongs to that node.
- **Visibility is not responsibility** — the node that sees a problem is not automatically the node that owns it.
- **Assistance is not transfer** — helping, reviewing, advising, or stabilizing does not by itself relocate ownership.
- **Ownership clarity** — responsibility remains anchored where the originating action, mandate, authority, benefit capture, or control resides.
- **Scope boundary** — directional assistance, scoped contribution, and full ownership remain distinct.
- **Authority match** — a node expected to carry responsibility must have authority to alter, refuse, halt, revise, or redistribute the relevant process.
- **Process visibility** — accountability requires sufficient access to production paths, source materials, transformations, tool operations, and decision traces.
- **Residual burden concentration** — unassigned responsibility accumulates around capable nodes that prevent visible failure.
- **Human judgment boundary** — AI assistance, document structuring, summarization, or comparison does not replace the human location of interpretation and judgment.

The model does not claim that capable actors should not help.

It distinguishes help from transfer, competence from ownership, and visibility from accountability.

## Structural Logic

Responsibility misalignment commonly follows a recurring sequence:

```text
problem emerges
→ a capable or visible node recognizes it
→ visibility creates responsibility pull
→ capacity is converted into duty
→ bounded contribution expands
→ accountability moves downstream
→ residual burden concentrates
→ misalignment stabilizes as workflow
```

The system may remain functional because unresolved burden is absorbed by the node most likely to prevent visible breakdown.

This makes responsibility drift difficult to detect.

The formal structure may say responsibility is distributed.

The operational structure may depend on concentrated absorption.

## Primary Misalignment Patterns

The model identifies several recurring patterns:

### Capacity-Based Overassignment

```text
capacity visible
→ responsibility expands
```

A capable node becomes responsible because it can carry the burden.

---

### Recognition-Based Transfer

```text
problem recognized
→ problem assigned
```

The node that notices a defect becomes expected to resolve it.

---

### Assistance-to-Ownership Drift

```text
bounded help
→ total task inheritance
```

A partial contribution expands into full responsibility without explicit transfer.

---

### Authority-Deficient Responsibility

```text
burden assigned
→ authority absent
```

A node is expected to carry consequences but lacks decision power, resources, or control.

---

### Visibility-Deficient Accountability

```text
accountability assigned
→ process hidden
```

A node is held responsible for outputs or decisions whose production path it cannot inspect.

---

### Final-Node Responsibility Concentration

```text
distributed production
→ final reviewer accountable
```

Many upstream actors, tools, interfaces, or processes contribute to an output, but responsibility concentrates at the final sign-off point.

---

### Residual Stabilizer Dependency

```text
formal responsibility unclear
→ capable stabilizer absorbs unresolved burden
```

The system functions because one node repeatedly absorbs unassigned work.

---

### Proxy-Based Responsibility Misassignment

```text
proxy indicates issue
→ proxy handler becomes responsible
```

The handler of a metric, dashboard, report, classification, or AI output becomes accountable for the underlying condition represented by the proxy.

---

### Tool-Assisted Responsibility Ambiguity

```text
AI produces output
→ human uses output
→ responsibility boundary unclear
```

Tool, user, platform, dataset, institution, and approver participate in production, while accountability is assigned only at the visible human layer.

## Distinction

Responsibility Alignment is not personal boundary discourse.

```text
personal boundary discourse
= refusal, emotional limit, interpersonal well-being, or individual comfort

responsibility alignment
= structural correspondence among ownership, scope, authority, visibility, capacity, burden, and accountability
```

Responsibility Alignment is not delegation.

```text
delegation
= explicit transfer with authority and scope

responsibility drift
= implicit transfer through visibility, capacity, urgency, proximity, or absorptive likelihood
```

Responsibility Alignment is not accountability alone.

```text
accountability
= who must answer for an outcome

responsibility alignment
= whether accountability is supported by ownership, authority, visibility, and capacity
```

Responsibility Alignment is not competence.

```text
competence
= ability to act

responsibility alignment
= proper location of responsibility
```

Responsibility Alignment is not negligence analysis.

```text
negligence
= failure to perform a clearly owned duty

responsibility misalignment
= responsibility is structurally uncertain, displaced, expanded, or absorbed
```

The question is not only:

```text
Who can solve this?
```

The question becomes:

```text
Who owns this, who has authority, who can see enough, who is accountable, and where is the burden actually landing?
```

## Application Scope

The Responsibility Alignment Model may apply wherever responsibility moves through multi-node systems.

Relevant contexts include:

- AI-generated output review
- human-in-the-loop systems
- agentic AI action traces
- institutional workflows
- expert review chains
- policy planning and review
- automated decision systems
- platform governance
- moderation systems
- dashboard or proxy-driven governance
- legal or administrative document review
- organizational coordination roles
- public communication workflows
- meaning-bearing systems with distributed production and concentrated sign-off

The model is especially relevant where production becomes distributed while validation, interpretation, correction, legal exposure, or public accountability concentrates downstream.

## Non-Applicability

The model should not be used for every case of discomfort, workload, conflict, refusal, or ordinary task difficulty.

It does not apply well when:

- responsibility is explicitly assigned and properly supported
- ownership, authority, visibility, capacity, and accountability are already aligned
- the actor is avoiding a known duty
- the issue is ordinary task difficulty rather than responsibility drift
- refusal would create direct harm under a clearly owned obligation
- no multi-node responsibility field exists
- the issue concerns preference rather than structural assignment
- accountability is symbolic but not consequential
- the analysis requires legal liability determination rather than structural mapping

The model should not be used to claim that capable actors should never help.

Its domain is narrower:

```text
misalignment between responsibility carried and responsibility structurally owned
```

## Naming Declaration

The **Responsibility Alignment Model** originates within the Meta-Writing Ecology as a structural model for describing how responsibility becomes distorted when it is assigned, assumed, displaced, or absorbed according to visibility, competence, proximity, urgency, or available capacity rather than actual ownership, authority, and accountable position.

The term **responsibility** in this model does not refer only to moral duty, legal liability, or formal job title. It refers to the extent to which a subject, role, institution, tool, or system node becomes expected to stabilize, complete, validate, absorb, repair, or answer for the consequences of a problem.

The term **alignment** does not refer to emotional comfort, team harmony, consensus, or general AI alignment. It refers to the structural correspondence among ownership, scope, authority, visibility, capacity, burden, and accountability.

The term **responsibility drift** refers to the movement of responsibility toward the node most likely to recognize, absorb, stabilize, or repair unresolved burden, even when formal ownership has not moved.

This model does not claim that capable actors should not help, nor that responsibility should be minimized. It distinguishes help from transfer, capacity from ownership, and visibility from accountability.

This model is not derived from self-help boundary discourse, productivity coaching, managerial delegation theory, burnout recovery slogans, refusal ethics, labor economics, AI alignment theory, principal–agent theory, or conventional accountability frameworks, although intersections may exist.

This document constitutes the first OSF external-facing version of the Responsibility Alignment Model as a structural framework for analyzing responsibility drift, capacity-based overassignment, authority mismatch, accountability displacement, residual burden concentration, and responsibility calibration in AI-mediated, institutional, platform, governance, and meaning-bearing systems.
