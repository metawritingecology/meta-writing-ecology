## Minimal Formulation

Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics describes the condition under which responsibility assignment is tested against the alignment of ownership, authority, capacity, visibility, scope, accountability, and burden.

It begins from three central distinctions:

```text
capacity
≠
ownership

visibility
≠
responsibility

assistance
≠
transfer
```

In compressed form:

```text
problem appears
→ visible node notices
→ capable node assists
→ assistance becomes expected
→ boundary remains unclear
→ accountability drifts
→ burden normalizes
→ responsibility misalignment stabilizes
```

Most compressed:

```text
The question is not only who can fix it.
The question is who owns the condition that made fixing necessary.
```

Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics does not dissolve accountability.

It asks whether accountability is structurally supported by ownership, authority, visibility, capacity, scope, and burden location.

---

## Core Characteristics

Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics treats responsibility as a structural relation rather than a personal trait, moral conclusion, legal determination, emotional boundary, or job-title assignment.

Its core characteristics include:

- **Ownership location** — which node legitimately owns the source condition, process, system, decision, or maintenance pathway.
- **Authority location** — which node has permission, mandate, or power to change the condition.
- **Capacity location** — which node can act, interpret, review, repair, stabilize, route, approve, reject, or explain.
- **Visibility location** — where the problem becomes observable through dashboard, workflow, review, documentation, user contact, or interface exposure.
- **Accountability position** — which node is expected to answer for the outcome.
- **Burden location** — where verification, correction, repair, coordination, maintenance, delay, explanation, or risk absorption lands.
- **Scope boundary** — whether the limits of responsibility are explicit, bounded, and reviewable.
- **Transfer boundary** — whether responsibility transfer has been explicitly authorized, scoped, supported, and made visible.
- **Residual responsibility** — the leftover burden that lands where formal roles, workflows, automation, or policies fail to contain it.
- **Repair capacity** — whether the assigned node can repair the source condition or only correct visible effects.

The framework applies when responsibility appears to land somewhere because a node is visible, proximate, competent, available, or capable of repair, rather than because the node owns, authorizes, benefits from, or can structurally repair the condition.

## Structural Logic

Responsibility misalignment often follows a recurring sequence:

```text
system output or failure appears
→ visibility lands on a node
→ capacity becomes activated
→ assistance occurs
→ boundary remains unspecified
→ accountability drifts
→ burden normalizes
→ source condition remains unresolved
→ misalignment stabilizes
```

The system may appear to have a responsibility structure.

In practice, it may have stabilized around burden absorption.

A node may become responsible not because responsibility was legitimately transferred, but because the node successfully noticed, stabilized, repaired, or absorbed the failure.

This makes responsibility drift difficult to detect.

The visible structure may say responsibility is assigned.

The operational structure may reveal that responsibility has been absorbed.

## Primary Misalignment Patterns

### 1. Capacity-Ownership Misalignment

A node can act, but does not own the condition.

```text
AI output fails
→ human reviewer can detect error
→ reviewer becomes treated as responsible
→ model / retrieval / workflow source remains elsewhere
```

The system mistakes capacity to catch failure for ownership of failure.

---

### 2. Visibility-Responsibility Drift

A node sees a problem first and becomes treated as responsible for it.

```text
dashboard reveals anomaly
→ operator sees it
→ operator must explain it
→ source condition lies outside operator authority
```

The system mistakes visibility for responsibility.

---

### 3. Assistance-to-Transfer Conversion

A node assists once or repeatedly, and the assistance becomes treated as responsibility transfer.

```text
expert stabilizes ambiguous case
→ system depends on expert again
→ expert becomes default owner of ambiguity
```

The system mistakes help for ownership transfer.

---

### 4. Human Review Burden Absorption

A human-in-the-loop role becomes the absorber of AI system uncertainty.

```text
model produces uncertain output
→ human reviewer validates
→ reviewer signs off
→ system treats review as accountability closure
```

The system substitutes human confirmation for structural responsibility alignment.

---

### 5. Accountability Without Authority

A node is held accountable for a condition it cannot change.

```text
frontline team must answer for failure
→ upstream system design remains inaccessible
→ team lacks authority to redesign process
```

Accountability becomes liability without agency.

---

### 6. Authority Without Visibility

A node has authority over a system but lacks visibility into downstream consequences.

```text
central policy controls workflow
→ downstream burden appears elsewhere
→ authority node cannot see absorption cost
```

Governance decisions detach from lived operational consequence.

---

### 7. Ownership Without Capacity

A node formally owns a condition but lacks the capacity to maintain or repair it.

```text
office is assigned responsibility
→ staffing / tools / authority insufficient
→ work shifts to informal actors
```

Formal ownership becomes symbolic while operational responsibility drifts.

---

### 8. Sign-Off Substitution

A signature or approval step is treated as responsibility resolution.

```text
AI-generated document
→ human signs
→ system treats sign-off as full accountability
→ production chain remains opaque
```

Sign-off substitutes for process traceability.

---

### 9. Proximity-Based Blame

The node closest to the failure becomes the blame surface.

```text
support worker receives complaint
→ support worker appears responsible
→ deeper system condition remains unassigned
```

Proximity converts into responsibility attribution.

---

### 10. Residual Burden Concentration

Unassigned or unresolved responsibility accumulates at one absorbing node.

```text
unclear ownership
→ repeated exceptions
→ one role keeps resolving them
→ residual responsibility concentrates
```

The absorbing node becomes necessary while not necessarily authorized.

---

### 11. Competence Penalty

A competent node becomes overburdened because it repeatedly resolves problems others cannot.

```text
competent reviewer catches failures
→ system routes more cases to reviewer
→ capacity becomes extraction surface
```

Competence becomes a reason for responsibility concentration.

---

### 12. Documentation Ownership Drift

The maintainer of documentation becomes responsible for unresolved system ambiguity.

```text
policy unclear
→ documentation maintainer clarifies
→ maintainer becomes expected to resolve future ambiguity
→ source authority remains elsewhere
```

Documentation access becomes mistaken for governance ownership.

## Distinction

Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics is not legal liability analysis.

```text
legal liability
= responsibility under law, contract, regulation, duty, or enforceable obligation

responsibility alignment diagnostics
= structural testing of ownership, authority, capacity, visibility, burden, and accountability location
```

It is not ethics.

```text
ethics
= evaluation of blame, justice, harm, legitimacy, or moral acceptability

responsibility alignment diagnostics
= mapping whether responsibility is structurally aligned or misaligned
```

It is not human oversight.

```text
human oversight
= human participation in monitoring, review, approval, or intervention

responsibility alignment diagnostics
= whether the human role can structurally carry the responsibility assigned to it
```

It is not responsibility diffusion alone.

```text
responsibility diffusion
= responsibility spreads too widely to locate

responsibility alignment diagnostics
= includes diffusion, but also examines concentration, displacement, absorption, transfer, and authority mismatch
```

It is not workflow design.

```text
workflow design
= process steps and role arrangements

responsibility alignment diagnostics
= whether those steps align responsibility with ownership, authority, capacity, visibility, and accountability
```

The central distinction is:

```text
can fix
≠
owns the condition

can see
≠
is responsible

can help
≠
has received transfer
```

Relevant contexts include:

- AI deployment review
- human-in-the-loop systems
- AI-assisted writing workflows
- AI-generated legal or administrative drafts
- model output verification
- search summary evaluation
- automated decision support
- agentic tool chains
- platform moderation
- policy review
- compliance workflows
- institutional sign-off chains
- documentation governance
- knowledge base maintenance
- dashboard monitoring
- public-sector decision processes
- organizational AI usage guidelines
- high-risk workflow review
- multi-role institutional procedures

The framework is especially relevant when a system can answer who touched the output, but cannot answer who owns the condition that made the output risky.

## AI-Mediated Responsibility Misalignment

AI-mediated systems intensify responsibility misalignment because production, review, deployment, use, verification, and accountability are often separated.

A generated output may be produced by:

```text
model behavior
prompt structure
retrieval layer
interface constraints
platform policy
user request
agentic tool action
institutional template
prior generated output
```

The output may then be reviewed, approved, accepted, cited, filed, deployed, or acted on by another node.

Misalignment occurs when this distributed chain collapses into a single visible responsible node.

A human who reviews an output may not own the model.

A user who requests an output may not control the system.

A signatory who approves a document may not see the generation chain.

A maintainer who edits documentation may not own the policy.

A moderator who applies a rule may not own the category system.

The diagnostic question is therefore not simply:

```text
Was a human involved?
```

The question becomes:

```text
What kind of involvement was it,
and did that involvement carry ownership, authority, visibility, capacity, and accountability together?
```

## Relationship to Adjacent Models

Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics is a diagnostics-facing extension of the [Responsibility Alignment Model](./responsibility-alignment-model.md).

The earlier model defines aligned responsibility as correspondence among legitimate ownership, bounded scope, sufficient authority, operational capacity, process visibility, and accountable position. Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics examines the patterns by which those dimensions separate.

- [Responsibility Alignment Model](./responsibility-alignment-model.md) — defines what aligned responsibility requires; Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics examines how misalignment becomes detectable.
- [Cost Visibility and Redistribution Model](./cost-visibility-redistribution.md) — asks whether burdens enter decision space; Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics asks whether the burden has landed on the node that owns and can govern it.
- [Benefit–Burden Allocation Regimes](./benefit-burden-allocation-regimes.md) — classifies how benefits and burdens are distributed; Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics examines whether responsibility, authority, and burden remain structurally aligned.
- Constraint Residue Accumulation Model — describes how prior failures become persistent controls; responsibility misalignment can produce residue when sign-off rules, review requirements, or escalation conditions are added without repairing ownership mismatch.
- Constraint Residue Governance — describes governance through accumulated failure traces; responsibility misalignment can place controls on visible reviewers or maintainers rather than repairing source conditions.
- Constraint Displacement after Capability Expansion — describes how expanded production relocates constraints downstream; Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics asks who becomes responsible after the constraint moves.
- Reality Consistency Model — includes subject-position consistency; Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics examines whether the reviewer, evaluator, user, signatory, or operator is placed into a role exceeding their visibility, authority, or capacity.
- [False Legibility Model](./false-legibility.md) — describes readable surfaces accepted before structural recognition; responsibility structures can become falsely legible when sign-off, review, or policy ownership appears to resolve accountability.
- [Premature Circulation Model](./premature-circulation-model.md) — describes movement before recognition, verification, or accountability stabilizes; responsibility misalignment may occur when outputs circulate first and responsibility is assigned reactively.
- [Observer–Representation Boundary](./observer-representation-boundary.md) — describes limits between what systems represent and what observers can see; responsibility misalignment intensifies when the accountable node can only see the representation, not the underlying condition.
- [Proxy Substitution](./proxy-substitution.md) — describes governance of a proxy rather than a referent; responsibility may become proxy-based when a node is held accountable for improving a metric, label, dashboard state, or review marker rather than the underlying referent.
- [Premature Coherence](./premature-coherence.md) — describes closure before sufficient differentiation; responsibility structures may appear coherent too early when a sign-off chain or human review step creates the impression of settled accountability.
- Irreversibility Conditions — describe no-return regimes after lost distinctions cannot be restored; responsibility misalignment may become difficult to reverse once burden absorption is normalized.
- Curvature as Interpretive Load — describes how accumulated coherence increases the effort required to revise distinctions; repeated responsibility attribution can create responsibility curvature.
- AI-Induced Semantic Deviation Under Pressure-Dense Texts — describes machine-mediated compression or reweighting of pressure-dense structures; responsibility misalignment may occur when downstream correctors become accountable for the transformation environment that produced deviation.

## Non-Applicability

Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics should not be used for every case of responsibility, assistance, review, disagreement, workload, or accountability.

It does not apply well when:

- ownership, authority, capacity, visibility, and accountability are already aligned
- responsibility transfer is explicit, bounded, authorized, and supported
- the node carrying responsibility has adequate authority and capacity
- assistance is clearly temporary and non-transferable
- review roles are properly scoped
- the system maintains traceable production and decision pathways
- burden allocation is visible and intentionally accepted
- responsibility is being evaluated through a legal, contractual, or normative framework rather than structural alignment
- the actor is avoiding a known duty
- the issue is ordinary task difficulty rather than responsibility drift
- no multi-node responsibility field exists

The framework should not be used to deny legitimate responsibility.

It should not be used to argue that capacity never creates obligation.

It should not be used to excuse roles from clearly assigned, authorized, and supported accountability.

Its domain is narrower:

```text
responsibility structures in which capacity, visibility, assistance, proximity, or burden absorption
are mistaken for ownership, authority, or legitimate accountability
```

## Naming Declaration

The term **Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics** originates within Meta-Writing Ecology as a cross-architecture and protocol orientation for describing how responsibility assignment, accountability position, burden location, authority, visibility, capacity, and ownership can be structurally examined in AI-mediated, institutional, platform, agentic, and meaning-bearing systems.

The term **responsibility alignment** refers to the condition in which responsibility corresponds to legitimate ownership, bounded scope, sufficient authority, operational capacity, process visibility, and accountable position.

The term **capacity-ownership misalignment** refers to the condition in which a node's ability to act, repair, review, or stabilize is treated as evidence that the node owns the source condition.

The term **visibility-responsibility drift** refers to the condition in which the node that sees a problem becomes treated as responsible for the problem.

The term **assistance-transfer boundary** refers to the distinction between helping with a condition and receiving responsibility for that condition.

The term **accountability calibration** refers to the structural adjustment required for accountability to correspond to ownership, authority, capacity, visibility, scope, and burden.

The term **burden absorption** refers to the condition in which a node carries verification, correction, maintenance, interpretive, coordination, or accountability burden without explicit or properly supported responsibility transfer.

This framework is adjacent to AI accountability, responsible AI, human-in-the-loop governance, organizational responsibility, platform governance, risk management, compliance workflows, and institutional accountability frameworks. It is not derived from those fields, although intersections may exist.

This document does not constitute legal analysis, compliance certification, ethical judgment, contractual responsibility assignment, professional liability determination, or a proprietary client-facing diagnostic workflow.

Within Meta-Writing Ecology, Responsibility Alignment Diagnostics functions as a diagnostics-facing extension of the Responsibility Alignment Model. It also intersects with Cost Visibility and Redistribution, Benefit-Burden Allocation Regimes, Constraint Residue Accumulation, Constraint Residue Governance, Constraint Displacement after Capability Expansion, Reality Consistency, False Legibility, Premature Circulation, Observer-Representation Boundary, Proxy Substitution, Premature Coherence, Irreversibility Conditions, Curvature as Interpretive Load, and AI-Induced Semantic Deviation Under Pressure-Dense Texts.

It names the condition under which systems must distinguish who can see or fix a problem from who owns, authorizes, benefits from, and can structurally repair the condition that produced it.
